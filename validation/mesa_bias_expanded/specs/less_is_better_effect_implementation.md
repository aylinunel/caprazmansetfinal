# Less-is-Better Effect: illustrative implementation

**Scope.** This is a hypothetical choice process for two explicitly supplied options, not a detector, diagnosis, population model, or empirically validated account. The task requires an explicit evaluation mode and two options with nonempty item values and a finite nonnegative total cost. Costs are debited rather than ignored.

For option *i*, total net value is `N_i = sum(item_values_i) - total_cost_i`; its mean net value is `A_i = N_i / item_count_i`. Under **joint** evaluation, the score is `N_i`. Under **separate** evaluation, the score is `(1-s)N_i + sA_i`, where `s` is the user-set intervention strength in `[0,1]`. The selected option maximizes that score (ties go to the first option). The reported metric is the selected option's **total net value** in synthetic net-value units, regardless of comparison mode. Thus a separate-evaluation preference for a compact, objectively lower-net-value option can lower the task metric; it is not merely a relabeled belief update.

**Reference and necessary condition.** Strength zero always compares total net values on the same task. The necessary condition for this modeled effect is separate evaluation; the boundary scenario switches to joint evaluation, removing that condition so every strength uses the same total-net comparison. The positive scenario has a compact option with net value 5.5 (mean 5.5) and a two-item bundle with net value 6 (mean 3); at strength .8 the compact option is selected, while at strength zero the larger bundle wins. The positive comparison therefore decreases the metric; the joint boundary comparison is equal to its strength-zero reference.

**Assumptions and limitations.** Item values and costs are hypothetical comparable units. Interpolating a total with a per-item mean mixes scales and is only a transparent toy intervention, not a calibrated coefficient or established psychological law. The task assumes the evaluation mode instead of inferring it, does not model real-world preference learning or presentation, and does not control all possible explanations for compact-option preference. Strength is not confidence, prevalence, or an empirical effect size. Passing executable tests is not empirical validation.
